\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A speech emotion recogniser trained on one corpus does markedly worse on
another, and the standard response is to align the two feature distributions
before training. The family of methods that does this --- standardisation,
CORAL, MMD minimisation and their descendants --- is well established, and the
usual evidence for a member of it is that applying it improves transfer and
reduces a measured discrepancy between the domains.

This paper runs that family end to end on one fixed protocol and asks what the
improvement is actually made of. The answer is not the one the framing
suggests. Alignment does help, substantially: between $+0.1208$ and $+0.1359$
macro-F1 for RAVDESS $\rightarrow$ CREMA-D and between $+0.1524$ and $+0.1961$
for CREMA-D $\rightarrow$ RAVDESS, against chance floors of $0.1665$ and
$0.1656$. But essentially all of it is available from per-dimension
standardisation, and the reason is that alignment removes the wrong
discrepancy.

\paragraph{The mechanism.} Decomposing the shift into label, marginal and
conditional components shows what the ladder is doing. Alignment drives the
marginal term down to $0.010\times$ its unaligned value while the conditional
term falls only to $0.07\times$; the ratio of conditional to marginal rises from
$0.14$ to $0.92$ in the forward direction. After alignment, what separates the
two corpora is almost entirely a difference in $P(y \mid x)$ --- exactly the
thing a covariate-shift correction assumes is absent and cannot repair. Label
shift is not the explanation either: the prior KL is $0.02235$ and $0.02541$
nats, and correcting for it \emph{hurts} in 238 of 240 cases while the same
estimators recover a planted shift. Two of the three standard shift families are
excluded by measurement, and the third accounts for what is left.

This explains the plateau rather than merely reporting it. The elaborate rungs
are not failing at their objective --- \texttt{mkmmd\_full} reaches the lowest
marginal discrepancy in both measurement geometries --- they are succeeding at
an objective that stopped binding after the first step.

\paragraph{What follows from it.} Three further results fall out, each of which
qualifies how the alignment literature's evidence should be read.

CORAL, at the regularisation its own source-side validation selects, is not
doing what its name describes. We derive analytically that as the shrinkage
$\varepsilon$ grows the CORAL map tends to a scalar rescale plus a mean shift,
and confirm it: at the selected $\varepsilon = 10$ the map is already $86\%$ of
the way to a pure scalar, and at $\varepsilon = 1000$ its scores land within
$0.0061$ of \texttt{mean\_shift} in both directions. A covariance-matching
method that has degenerated to a mean shift will still be reported as covariance
matching.

Source-side validation cannot detect whether alignment is needed. It separates
\texttt{none} from \texttt{zscore} by $0.0018$ where the target separates them
by $0.13$. It is not a noisy estimate of the target ordering; on this decision it
is nearly blind to it, and it selects the unaligned configuration on 2 of 5
seeds. One of those seeds returns $0.1508$ --- \textbf{below the $0.1665$ chance
floor} --- from a protocol that touched no target labels at any point. The gap
between validated and oracle selection, $0.1399$ $[0.0231, 0.2568]$ forward, is
larger than the entire benefit of alignment.

Finally, the discrepancy--transfer relationship that justifies the whole family
does not have a well-defined sign. Measured in each method's own geometry the
correlation is $\rho = -0.444$ $[-0.505, -0.383]$; measured in a single fixed
reference frame, on the same features, layers and scores, it is $+0.142$
$[+0.062, +0.221]$. The signs are opposite and neither interval covers zero.
Which one a study obtains is fixed by a measurement choice made before any
transfer result is seen, and usually not reported. The $\varepsilon$ sweep
supplies a case where the correct answer is known independently, and one frame
gets it wrong.

\paragraph{What this paper is and is not.} It is not a new alignment method and
does not report a new best score; the absolute numbers here are modest. It is an
account of why a widely used family behaves as it does, on a protocol built so
that the account can be checked: pre-registered comparisons, speaker-disjoint
splits, chance floors beside every score, an equal search budget across
classifier families, and a validated-versus-oracle distinction drawn explicitly
rather than blurred. Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility} lists the seven claims we
made during this work and later withdrew, which is part of the same commitment.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A mechanism for the alignment plateau.} The residual shift after
    alignment is $92\%$ conditional; label shift is negligible and correcting it
    hurts in 238 of 240 cases while recovering a planted shift.
    (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition})
  \item \textbf{The gain is the first rung.} Alignment is worth $+0.12$ to
    $+0.20$, and no aligned rung is shown to beat per-dimension standardisation
    in either direction. (Section~\ref{sec:results-ladder})
  \item \textbf{CORAL degenerates at its selected regularisation.} Derived
    analytically and confirmed to within $0.0061$.
    (Section~\ref{sec:results-eps})
  \item \textbf{Source-side validation cannot detect whether alignment is
    needed}, and once returns a score below the chance floor.
    (Section~\ref{sec:results-selection})
  \item \textbf{Discrepancy--transfer correlations reverse sign with the
    measurement frame}, so the relationship is not identified until the frame is
    specified. (Section~\ref{sec:results-frames})
\end{enumerate}
