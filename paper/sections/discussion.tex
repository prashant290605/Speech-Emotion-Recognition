\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the mechanism implies for practice}
\label{sec:discussion-practice}

The decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} is the result the rest of
the paper follows from, and it has a direct practical reading.

\paragraph{Why standardisation suffices.} Alignment buys between $+0.1208$ and
$+0.1359$ forward and between $+0.1524$ and $+0.1961$ reverse, against chance
floors of $0.1665$ and $0.1656$. Essentially all of it is available from
per-dimension standardisation: bounding simultaneously over the four contrasts,
the largest advantage any other rung could hold over \texttt{zscore} is
$+0.0148$ forward and $0.0000$ reverse. No rung is shown to beat standardisation
in either direction. Matching covariance, or matching higher-order structure
through a learned map, does not pay. The reason is visible in the discrepancy columns rather than in the scores.
Marginal discrepancy falls to $0.010\times$ its unaligned value while the
conditional term falls only to $0.07\times$, and the ratio of conditional to
marginal rises from $0.14$ to $0.92$ forward and from $0.29$ to $0.53$ reverse.
The elaborate rungs succeed at what they optimise. What they optimise stops
being the binding constraint almost immediately.

\paragraph{Why marginal alignment cannot help further.} Once $92\%$ of the
residual discrepancy is conditional, a method that matches marginals has almost
nothing left to remove. This is not a statement about tuning or about our
particular implementations: \texttt{mkmmd\_full} reaches the lowest marginal
discrepancy in \emph{both} measurement geometries and is never the best rung,
and in the reverse direction it is the worst aligned one. A method can win
decisively on its own objective and lose on the task, and here it does.

The point generalises beyond these rungs. Covariate-shift correction is
justified by an assumption --- that $P(y \mid x)$ is stable across domains ---
which is testable and, in this corpus pair, false in the direction that matters.
Label shift, the other standard alternative, is not the explanation either: the
prior KL is $0.02235$ and $0.02541$ nats, and correcting for it \emph{hurts} in
238 of 240 cases, by $0.013$ to $0.24$ macro-F1, while the same estimators
recover a planted shift. Two of the three standard shift families are ruled out
by measurement rather than by argument. The third is what remains.

\paragraph{What a correct selection protocol would have to measure.} If the
binding constraint is conditional shift, then a protocol that scores candidates
by how well their marginals match is scoring the wrong thing --- and this is not
hypothetical, because it is what discrepancy-guided selection does. A protocol
that could work would need a target-side signal that is sensitive to
$P(y \mid x)$ and does not require target labels. We do not have one to offer,
and we think its absence is the real obstacle to unsupervised cross-corpus
adaptation, more than the choice of alignment family. What we can say is what
such a signal must survive: it must separate configurations that source-side
validation cannot, and Section~\ref{sec:results-selection} shows how wide that
gap is.

\subsection{Selection is where this actually bites}
\label{sec:discussion-selection}

The headline numbers are what a practitioner obtains: $0.3156$
$[0.1880, 0.4432]$ forward against a floor of $0.1665$, and $0.4994$
$[0.4592, 0.5395]$ reverse against $0.1656$. The oracle configurations reach
$0.4555$ and $0.5680$, leaving gaps of $0.1399$ $[0.0231, 0.2568]$ and $0.0687$
$[0.0090, 0.1283]$. \textbf{The forward gap is larger than the entire benefit of
alignment.} Choosing the configuration is a bigger problem than choosing the
alignment method, and the literature's attention is distributed the other way
around.

The mechanism of the failure is specific and worth stating plainly.
Source-side validation separates \texttt{none} from \texttt{zscore} by $0.0018$
--- $0.7304$ against $0.7322$ --- while the target separates them by $0.13$.
Source validation is not a noisy estimate of the target ordering; on this
decision it is nearly blind to it. It therefore selects \texttt{none} on 2 of 5
forward seeds, returning $0.2803$ and $0.1508$. \textbf{The second of those is
below the $0.1665$ chance floor.} A practitioner following an unimpeachable
protocol --- no target labels touched at any point --- can land beneath chance
on a corpus pair where a good configuration reaches $0.4555$, and would have no
way of knowing from source-side evidence that anything had gone wrong.

This is also the argument for reporting floors on every number. Without the
floor beside it, $0.1508$ reads as a weak result rather than as a failure.

\subsection{Testing the conditional-shift explanation directly}
\label{sec:discussion-testing}

Our evidence for conditional shift is a residual: we measure the marginal and
label-shift terms, find both small after alignment, and attribute what remains
to the conditional. The falsifiable prediction we could test --- that
label-shift correction should not help --- passed, and passed sharply. But a
residual argument is weaker than a direct one, and we say what a direct test
would take.

It would need paired data: the same speakers, or the same sentences, recorded
under both corpora's conditions, so that $P(y \mid x)$ can be estimated
separately in each domain without the corpus difference and the speaker
difference being confounded. Failing that, per-utterance perceptual ratings from
multiple annotators would let the conditional be compared where the label is a
distribution rather than a point. Neither exists for this pair, which is why the
argument here is a residual one, and the attribution should be read as the
best-supported explanation of these measurements rather than as a directly
observed quantity.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Two corpora, one language, two directions.} Every result is a statement
about RAVDESS and CREMA-D in English. We expect the mechanism --- alignment
removing the marginal term while the conditional term binds --- to appear
elsewhere, but we have not shown that, and a corpus pair with a genuinely large
prior difference would test the label-shift half of the decomposition much
harder than this one does.

\textbf{Both corpora are acted.} Neither contains spontaneous speech. Acted
emotion is more prototypical and more clearly separated than spontaneous
emotion, so the absolute scores here are likely optimistic relative to
spontaneous data. Whether the \emph{mechanism} transfers is genuinely open: the
conditional shift between an acted and a spontaneous corpus may be larger still,
which would strengthen the argument, or a different term may dominate.

\textbf{Modest absolute performance.} Validated macro-F1 of $0.3156$ and
$0.4994$ clears chance comfortably but falls well short of in-domain SER. We
report a mechanism, not a system, and nothing here should be read as a
recommended pipeline.

\textbf{The reverse direction is less precisely measured.} RAVDESS as target
supplies $624$ test utterances against CREMA-D's $3677$ to $3690$, so reverse
intervals are wider throughout --- for the among-rung bound, about $1.8$ times.
Both directions are matched-$n$ on the source side, so the asymmetry in
\emph{difficulty} is not a data-quantity effect; the asymmetry in
\emph{precision} is.

\textbf{The Transformer arm is reduced-seed.} Two seeds, the primary direction
only, one inner-grid setting per rung. It is never pooled with the five-seed
families and its intervals are correspondingly wider.

\textbf{The MK-MMD rungs frequently fall back.} Optimisation reverts to its
warm start in $64.9\%$ of \texttt{mkmmd\_diag} runs and $55.2\%$ of
\texttt{mkmmd\_full} runs, so in the majority of cells the fitted map \emph{is}
the CORAL or diagonal warm start. This qualifies the negative result about
higher-moment matching: we have shown that this optimiser, at this budget, does
not beat standardisation, which is weaker than showing no such map could. A
better-optimised MK-MMD might do better --- though it would still be optimising
a marginal objective against a conditional residual, which is the deeper reason
we do not expect it to.
