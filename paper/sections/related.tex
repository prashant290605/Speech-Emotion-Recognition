\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Cross-corpus speech emotion recognition}
\label{sec:related-crosscorpus}

That emotion recognition degrades when the test corpus is not the training
corpus is old enough to have been systematised: cross-corpus evaluation was
already being posed as a question about variances across corpora, and about
strategies for absorbing them, fifteen years ago \cite{schuller2010cross}. The
responses since have been broadly of three kinds. Transfer learning and
fine-tuning move a model trained on one corpus toward another
\cite{naderi2023cross}. Multi-task and auxiliary-objective formulations try to
learn a representation that is not corpus-specific in the first place
\cite{fu2023cross}. Training-strategy work changes what the model sees rather
than what it computes \cite{pastor2023cross}. Domain adaptation proper --- the
family this paper is about --- appears in both adversarial \cite{latif2023self}
and feature-alignment \cite{naeeni2025feature} forms, the latter on the same
class of self-supervised encoder used here.

Most of this work is evaluated on some subset of a small pool of corpora, of
which IEMOCAP \cite{busso2008iemocap}, RAVDESS \cite{livingstone2018ryerson}
and CREMA-D \cite{cao2014crema} are the most frequent. Two properties of the
resulting literature matter here. First, the reported quantity is nearly always
a gain: some method, on some corpus pair, over some baseline. Second, the
baseline, the pair, the label mapping and the split policy vary between papers,
so the gains are not directly commensurable
[CITE: a survey or meta-analysis of cross-corpus SER documenting how corpus
pairs, label mappings, baselines and split policies vary across the literature;
needed to support the claim that reported gains are not commensurable]. A
consequence is that the field has many measurements of how much a given method
helps, and few of \emph{why} it helps or of what a simpler method would have
delivered on the same setup.

\subsection{Feature-space alignment under covariate shift}
\label{sec:related-alignment}

Alignment methods rest on a covariate-shift assumption: the marginal feature
distribution differs between corpora while the conditional distribution of the
label given the features does not
[CITE: the standard covariate-shift formulation and the importance-weighting
correction that follows from it; needed to state the assumption and its
consequence precisely]. Theoretical support is usually taken from bounds in
which target error is controlled by source error plus a divergence between the
domains --- and, in the standard result, that divergence is
\emph{classifier-induced}: it is defined over the symmetric difference of the
hypothesis class, and so depends on which hypotheses the learner can express
[CITE: the domain-adaptation generalisation bound whose divergence term is
defined over the symmetric difference hypothesis space and is estimable from
finite unlabelled samples. Verify that the divergence really is
hypothesis-class-dependent in the version cited, since the argument in
Section~\ref{sec:results-frames} turns on it]. This distinction is easy to lose
and matters here: a kernel MMD at a fixed bandwidth is not a
hypothesis-dependent quantity, so reducing it is not the same as reducing the
term the bound is stated over.

The methods form a natural ladder, ordered by how many moments they match.
Per-dimension standardisation matches first moments. CORAL matches second
moments by whitening the source and recolouring it to the target covariance, in
a closed form [CITE: the original closed-form CORAL, distinct from the deep
variant below] and as a differentiable training loss \cite{sun2016deep}.
MMD-based methods minimise a kernel two-sample statistic
\cite{gretton2012kernel}, in single-kernel [CITE: deep-network domain adaptation
minimising a single-kernel MMD between hidden-layer distributions] and
multi-kernel [CITE: the multi-kernel MMD formulation and its use as a domain
adaptation objective] forms, and so match higher-order structure in principle.
Adversarial approaches [CITE: domain-adversarial training with a gradient
reversal layer or an equivalent discriminator objective] pursue the same target
through a learned critic rather than a closed-form statistic.

Three things this literature does not usually do bear directly on our results.
It rarely runs the whole ladder, so the share of the gain available from the
cheapest rung is seldom reported [CITE: an ablation-practice survey; if no
source supports this as a general claim, narrow the sentence to the specific
papers cited above]. It rarely fixes the geometry in which the discrepancy is
measured: kernel bandwidth is typically set by the median heuristic
[CITE: the median heuristic for kernel bandwidth selection], which makes the
statistic a function of the very rescaling the method applies. And it states the
covariate-shift assumption without testing it, even though the alternatives ---
conditional shift [CITE: domain adaptation under conditional or target shift,
where the class-conditional distribution itself changes] and label shift
[CITE: label-shift correction by inverting a source confusion matrix; the same
source is needed in Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}] --- have
well-developed literatures of their own and are separately estimable.

\subsection{Self-supervised representations, and where emotion lives in them}
\label{sec:related-ssl}

Self-supervised encoders \cite{baevski2020wav2vec,hsu2021hubert,chen2022wavlm}
supply the features for most current SER systems, including the ones cited
above. How a stack of transformer layers is reduced to a single vector is a
choice, and the two common defaults are the final layer and a task-learned
weighted sum over layers [CITE: the benchmark that established the learned
layer-weighting convention for downstream speech tasks]. Layer-wise probing has
shown that what these encoders carry is not distributed uniformly across depth,
and that the most informative layers are not the last [CITE: layer-wise analysis
of self-supervised speech representations, establishing that phonetic and
word-level information peaks in intermediate layers]; comparable analyses exist
for paralinguistic targets [CITE: layer-wise or probing analysis for emotion or
another paralinguistic task specifically. If no such analysis exists, delete
this clause rather than stretching a phonetic result to cover emotion].

Layer choice and alignment are, however, usually studied apart. That separation
is why a result like ours --- that the layer decision is worth more than any
choice \emph{among} alignment methods --- is available to be found rather than
already known.

\subsection{Protocol: selection, floors, and what a reported number means}
\label{sec:related-protocol}

Cross-corpus transfer is by construction a setting with no labelled target data,
which makes model selection part of the problem rather than a detail of it. The
options are source-side validation, or one of the transfer-aware alternatives
such as importance-weighted cross-validation and reverse validation
[CITE: importance-weighted cross-validation for model selection under covariate
shift, and reverse or transfer cross-validation as its unsupervised counterpart].
Work that instead reports the best configuration found on the target is
reporting an oracle, and the distinction is not always drawn
[CITE: a methodological critique of tuning on the test domain in transfer
learning or domain adaptation; a general machine-learning statement of the
problem is acceptable if none is specific to SER].

Two further conventions bear on interpretation. Macro-F1 on an imbalanced
multi-class problem has a chance floor well above zero which depends on the
realised test priors, so a score reported without its floor cannot be read
[CITE: a source recommending explicit chance-level or permutation baselines when
reporting classification metrics]. And speaker-independent splits are necessary
because speaker identity is recoverable from these representations
[CITE: evidence that speaker identity is recoverable from self-supervised speech
representations, or that speaker-dependent splits inflate SER scores]. Benchmark
efforts have begun to standardise both \cite{ma2024emobox}.

\subsection{Position of this work}
\label{sec:related-position}

This paper is not a new alignment method and does not report a new best score.
It takes an existing and widely used family --- the ladder of
Section~\ref{sec:related-alignment} --- runs all of it under one fixed protocol,
and asks three questions the work above mostly does not: where the gain actually
comes from, whether the assumption underneath the family holds, and whether the
quantity the family optimises is well defined as it is measured. The answers
are, in order: the first rung; no, because the residual shift is conditional
rather than marginal; and not until a measurement frame is fixed. The nearest
precedent is the benchmarking strand represented by \cite{ma2024emobox}, which
shares the aim of making cross-corpus numbers comparable but pursues it by
standardising the evaluation rather than by decomposing the effect.
